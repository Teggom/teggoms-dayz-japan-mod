# Named landmarks W: Kyoto, Osaka, Nara, Lake Biwa and the Nakasendō (1730)

Research agent LM-W, 2026-09-29. Text only. Sister file: LM-E (Edo and the Tōkaidō east of Ōtsu).

**What this is.** Named, one-off places a traveller in 1730 knew by name, each a candidate "hero site" (the role the
prison, castles or airfield play on Chernarus). Generic building types are in `research/buildings/BUILDING_LIST.md`
and outdoor features in `research/outdoor/*.md`. This file reuses them (see Cross-references at the end).

**Era rule (binding).** Did it exist, or still stand, in 1730? Old is fine.
- `IN`: stood in 1730 more or less as described.
- `REBUILT`: today's building is later. The entry says what stood in 1730.
- `[not yet in 1730: date]`: built or began later.
- `[gone by 1730: date]`: destroyed before 1730. Listed only when the ruin, site or story still mattered.
- `(verify)`: from memory or a single weak source; re-check before modelling.

**Fame scale.** `national`: known across Japan, in guidebooks, plays or prints. `regional`: known along the road or in the
home province. `local`: known in the town. Evidence uses guides printed *before or near* 1730 where possible:
- *Kyō warabe* (1658), *Kyō habutae* (1685) and *Yōshū fushi* (1686): Kyoto guides and gazetteer
- *Tōkaidō meisho-ki* (c.1660)
- *Naniwa kagami* (1680) and *Setsuyō gundan* (1701): Osaka
- Bashō's *Sarashina kikō* (1688)
- Kaempfer's journeys (1691–92)
- Kaibara Ekiken's *Kisoji no ki* (1709)
- Chikamatsu's plays (1703–1724)

Later works (*Miyako meisho zue* 1780, *Settsu meisho zue* 1796–98, *Kisoji meisho zue* 1805, Eisen and
Hiroshige's Kisokaidō prints 1835–38) are cited for *form only*.

**Entry format.**
- **1730**: what stood, its layout, scale and look.
- **Era**: its status.
- **Fame**: how widely it was known, and the evidence.
- **Play**: gameplay use and set size (single building / compound / district / terrain).
- **Src**: source note.

**Count: 115 entries** (Kyoto 34, Osaka 17, Nara/Kōya/Yoshino/Ise 7, Lake Biwa 19, Nakasendō 38). Several
entries bundle a small cluster of named sites (e.g. Hōkō-ji + Ear Mound + Toyokuni ruin), so named places exceed 115.

## == Kyoto (Miyako / Kyō) ==

Context for the whole city:
- Two great fires frame 1730. The **Hōei fire (1708)** burned the palace and much of the centre; the city had been rebuilt by 1730.
- The **Tenmei fire (1788)** later burned about 80% of the city, including the palace, Nijō's honmaru palace and Higashi Honganji.
- So a building "rebuilt after 1788" usually means 1730 had its predecessor.
- **The Nishijin fire broke out in the anchor year itself** (Kyōhō 15, 6th month 20th day, i.e. August 1730). It destroyed about 3,790 houses and a reported 30,000+ looms.

### Imperial Palace (Kinri gosho / Dairi) and the court-noble quarter (kuge-machi): central-north Kyoto
- **1730**: The palace had been rebuilt in 1709 after the Hōei fire.
  - Layout: a walled rectangle of cypress-bark-roofed halls around white-gravel courts. The Shishinden (ceremonial hall) faced a court with the left cherry and right orange trees; the Seiryōden (emperor's residence) stood behind.
  - Around it lay the retired emperor's palace (Sentō gosho) and a dense ring of ~130–140 court-noble compounds behind plain walls (count assumed).
  - This was the "old" Edo-period layout. The antiquarian Heian-revival rebuild came after 1788.
- **Era**: REBUILT. The present halls date from 1855. In 1730 the **1709 Hōei rebuild** stood.
- **Fame**: national, and not open to commoners except at a few festivals. Kaempfer saw only its walls.
- **Play**: walled, gated compound with a restricted core; high-tier "court" loot (documents, lacquer, silk); guards. Set size: compound, very large (palace ~ 250 × 450 m (verify)) with the kuge district as a surrounding walled grid.
- **Src**: Kyoto Gosho history (Imperial Household Agency); Hōei 1708 and Tenmei 1788 fires; ja.wiki 京都御所.

### Nijō Castle (Nijō-jō): central-west Kyoto, the shogun's Kyoto seat
- **1730**: A full flatland castle with a double moat and stone walls.
  - **The five-storey keep was standing.** It had been brought from Fushimi castle in the 1620s rebuild for Emperor Go-Mizunoo's 1626 visit.
  - The Ninomaru palace (1603/1626) had its long zig-zag chain of halls and "nightingale floors".
  - There was also a honmaru palace, and the Kara-mon gate.
  - The castle was largely empty: no shogun visited between 1634 and 1863. It was kept by a garrison commander (Nijō zaiban).
- **Era**: IN.
  - The keep was lost to lightning in 1750 `[gone after 1730]`.
  - The honmaru palace burned in 1788. Today's honmaru palace was moved in from the palace in 1893 `[not yet]`.
  - Ieyasu's original keep had gone to Yodo castle (see Yodo).
- **Fame**: national. It is on every Kyoto city screen and map.
- **Play**: top-tier Kyoto castle, with a walled moat compound, gates as chokepoints, and a keep as lookout. Set size: compound (~500 × 400 m).
- **Src**: Nijō Castle official history; en/ja.wiki; jcastle.

### Kiyomizu-dera: Higashiyama hillside, east Kyoto
- **1730**: The main hall of 1633 stood on its famous timber-lattice stage (kake-zukuri) about 13 m over the ravine. With it were:
  - the red Niōmon and West Gate
  - a three-storey pagoda (1632)
  - the Otowa waterfall spring below
- The approach was lined with tea houses and pottery shops (the seed of Kiyomizu-yaki). "Jumping off the stage at Kiyomizu" was already a proverb: vow-jumps are recorded through the Edo period (verify count).
- **Era**: IN (the 1633 rebuild is still today's building).
- **Fame**: national (all Kyoto guides from *Kyō warabe* on).
- **Play**: famous viewpoint over the whole basin; the stage is a sniper perch; the spring is water; the slope streets are a mid-tier shopping district. Set size: compound + approach street.
- **Src**: Kiyomizu-dera official; en.wiki; BUILDING_LIST "Worship-stage hall (kake-zukuri)".

### Great Buddha Hall of Hōkō-ji (Kyō no Daibutsu), with its bell, the Ear Mound and the Toyokuni ruin: south-east Kyoto
- **1730**: The largest wooden building in Japan stood here.
  - The hall was about 49 m high on a ~88 × 54 m plan, bigger than Nara's.
  - Inside was a ~19 m gilt **wooden** Buddha (1667). It replaced bronze statues lost to the 1596 and 1662 earthquakes.
  - Beside it hung the great bell of 1614. Its inscription "Kokka ankō" was the Tokugawa pretext for the Osaka campaign.
  - Across the road was the **Mimizuka (Ear Mound, 1597)**, a grassed tumulus holding noses and ears cut from Korean and Chinese dead.
  - Up the hill on Amidagamine lay the ruin of Hideyoshi's Toyokuni shrine, suppressed by the Tokugawa in 1615.
- **Era**: IN in 1730.
  - Hall and statue burned by lightning 12 August 1798 `[gone after 1730]`.
  - The bell survives.
  - The Toyokuni shrine buildings were already a `[gone by 1730: 1615]` ruin.
- **Fame**: national. Kaempfer describes the Daibutsu. It was a standard stop with Sanjūsangendō next door.
- **Play**: the city's single biggest silhouette, which a player can't see in modern Kyoto. The hall is a vast open interior. The mound and ruin carry strong story. Set size: compound (hall precinct ~ 200 m square) + mound.
- **Src**: en.wiki Hōkō-ji (Kyoto); Shinto Wiki "Great Buddha Hall of Hōkō-ji"; KVG "Tragic tale of the Kyoto Great Buddha".

### Sanjūsangendō (Rengeō-in): south-east Kyoto, next to the Daibutsu
- **1730**: A 120 m long, low hall of 1266 with 33 bays between its pillars. It holds 1,001 standing Kannon statues.
- The west veranda was the course for the **tōshiya** endurance archery contest (arrows shot the full length). Wasa Daihachirō's record of 8,133 hits in 24 hours (1686) was still fresh.
- **Era**: IN (1266 building).
- **Fame**: national. The tōshiya was the most famous archery contest in Japan, and Edo had a copy hall.
- **Play**: long straight hall = firing lane; archery-themed loot; next to the Daibutsu for a "south-east temple cluster". Set size: single building (very long) + walled yard.
- **Src**: Rengeō-in official; BUILDING_LIST "Long-range through-shooting hall (tōshiya)".

### Tō-ji (Kyō-ō-gokoku-ji) and its five-storey pagoda: south Kyoto, by the old Rashōmon line
- **1730**: The 1644 pagoda (Iemitsu's rebuild) was about 55 m tall, the tallest wooden pagoda in Japan. It was the first thing travellers saw coming up from Osaka or Fushimi. Around it stood:
  - the Kondō (1603)
  - the Kōdō lecture hall with its esoteric statue mandala
  - the Kodō/Daishi-dō
  - a monthly market day on the 21st (Kōbō-san)
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national. The pagoda was the symbol of Kyoto's south entry.
- **Play**: skyline beacon at the south gate of the city; a climbable lookout if we allow ladders. Set size: compound (~250 m square).
- **Src**: Tō-ji official; en.wiki; BUILDING_LIST "Pagoda".

### Rashōmon site and the old Suzaku line: south Kyoto
- **1730**: Nothing but a name, a stone-less field and the legend of Rashōmon and its oni. The south city limit was the Odoi earthwork (Hideyoshi, 1591), partly levelled by 1730 (verify extent).
- **Era**: `[gone by 1730: Heian period]`.
- **Fame**: national (legend, Noh).
- **Play**: story marker only (a ruined gate base as an optional prop); the Odoi earthwork-and-moat remnants are a good city edge. Set size: prop / terrain.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 羅城門, 御土居.

### Kinkaku-ji (Rokuon-ji), the Golden Pavilion: north-west Kyoto
- **1730**: The original 1397 three-storey pavilion stood on its mirror pond (Kyōko-chi) with pine islands. The gilding had largely weathered by the Edo period (verify extent).
- **Era**: IN in 1730 (original). The original was burned by arson in 1950; the present building is 1955 `[the present building is not the 1730 one]`.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: pond garden + small pavilion = quiet, high-value "treasure" site; water. Set size: compound (garden ~ 300 m).
- **Src**: Rokuon-ji official; en.wiki; U_URBAN "Named landmark gardens".

### Ginkaku-ji (Jishō-ji), the Silver Pavilion: north-east Kyoto
- **1730**: The 1489 two-storey Kannon-den (never silvered) and the Tōgu-dō stood by a moss-and-pond garden at the foot of Higashiyama.
- **Era**: IN (original buildings). The raked sand terrace and sand cone (Ginshadan, Kōgetsudai) are probably Edo-period additions; whether they existed in 1730 is uncertain (verify).
- **Fame**: national but lesser than Kinkaku.
- **Play**: small high-value garden temple; start of the Higashiyama temple walk. Set size: compound.
- **Src**: Jishō-ji official; ja.wiki 銀閣寺 (向月台 dating).

### Nishi Honganji: west of Horikawa, south-central Kyoto
- **1730**: This was the head temple of the Honganji branch of True Pure Land. Its two giant halls stood side by side facing east: the Founder's Hall (Goeidō, 1636) and an Amida hall.
  - Other buildings: Hideyoshi-era Momoyama pieces, including the carved Kara-mon and the Hiunkaku pavilion (tradition says from Jurakudai), and the Shiro-shoin.
  - Around it was a sprawling temple town (jinaichō) of Shinshū supply shops: rosaries, altars, Buddhist goods.
- **Era**: IN.
  - The Goeidō is the 1636 building.
  - The present Amida-dō is 1760 `[not yet]`; an earlier Amida hall stood in 1730.
  - Nishi Honganji escaped the 1788 fire.
- **Fame**: national (the largest sect's headquarters).
- **Play**: enormous tatami halls = big open interiors; pilgrim crowds; mid-high loot. Set size: compound (~300 × 250 m) + monzen district.
- **Src**: Nishi Honganji official; BUILDING_LIST sect modifier "True Pure Land".

### Higashi Honganji: east of Nishi Honganji, south-central Kyoto
- **1730**: The rival Ōtani-branch head temple (founded 1602), with the same two-giant-hall plan and a large moated precinct. It held the Shōsei-en (Kikoku-tei) garden villa (1653) two blocks east.
- **Era**: REBUILT.
  - Burned in 1788, 1823, 1858 and 1864. Today's halls are 1895.
  - In 1730 the pre-1788 17th-century halls stood; their form is uncertain (verify).
  - The Shōsei-en garden layout was in place in 1730; its buildings were later.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: twin of Nishi Honganji, one kit, two sites. Set size: compound.
- **Src**: Higashi Honganji official history (fires 1788/1823/1858/1864, rebuild 1895).

### Chion-in: Higashiyama, east Kyoto
- **1730**: This was the Jōdo sect head temple.
  - The Sanmon (1621) is the largest temple gate in Japan, about 24 m high.
  - The vast Mieidō (1639) stood on a terrace above it.
  - The great bell (1636) needed a gang of monks to ring.
  - The "Seven Wonders", such as the left-behind umbrella in the eaves and the nightingale floors, were already told (verify dating of the list).
- **Era**: IN (1621/1639 buildings survive).
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: the gate is a monumental chokepoint and lookout; the terrace is a strongpoint over Gion. Set size: compound.
- **Src**: Chion-in official; BUILDING_LIST "Main gate, two-storey (sanmon)".

### Yasaka Shrine (Gion-sha / Gion Kanshin-in): head of Shijō, east Kyoto
- **1730**:
  - The honden of 1654 is huge, with the worship hall under one roof (Gion-zukuri).
  - The vermilion west tower gate (1497) faced down Shijō street.
  - The shrine was in full syncretic form, as Gion Kanshin-in, with Buddhist halls in the precinct.
  - The **Gion festival** floats (yamaboko) were stored in the downtown wards.
- **Era**: IN (1654 honden; the gate is 1497, with a later rebuild in part).
- **Fame**: national. The Gion festival was the best-known festival in Japan.
- **Play**: anchor of the Gion district; the festival float stores = unusual loot rooms. Set size: compound.
- **Src**: Yasaka Jinja official; BUILDING_LIST "Festival float store (yama-hoko-gura)".

### Gion, the Shijō riverbed theatres and Pontochō: along Shijō and the Kamo river, east Kyoto
- **1730**:
  - **Gion**: tea-house streets in front of Yasaka shrine had grown from the 1660s–1710s into the city's liveliest quarter. It was unlicensed but tolerated.
  - **Shijō-kawara**: licensed kabuki and puppet theatres with drum towers stood on both sides of the Shijō bridge (the ancestor of the Minami-za).
  - **Pontochō**: a narrow strip of tea houses and inns along the Kamo, built on reclaimed bank from 1670 and developed as a pleasure street from 1712.
  - In summer the riverbed held evening-cool (suzumi) stalls and platforms.
- **Era**: IN. Pontochō's geisha licence came in 1859 `[not yet]`; in 1730 it was a teahouse street for Takase-river boatmen and travellers.
- **Fame**: national (Gion, Shijō theatres); regional (Pontochō).
- **Play**: dense night district: lantern light, narrow alleys, theatre interiors; mid-tier loot, social hub. Set size: district.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 祇園, 先斗町 (1712); BUILDING_LIST "Kabuki theatre", "Theatre tea house".

### Shimabara pleasure quarter: west Kyoto, near the Tanba road
- **1730**: This was Kyoto's only licensed quarter, moved here in 1641. It was walled and moated, with a single great gate (ōmon) and a willow at the gate.
  - The ageya (banquet houses) included the **Sumiya**, with a 1641 core and later wings, and lavish interiors.
  - The okiya of the tayū courtesans were the Wachigaiya type.
  - By 1730 it was already losing trade to Gion, but it kept a strong literary culture (the Shimabara haikai circle).
- **Era**: IN (Sumiya survives, core 1641; the present gate is a later rebuild (verify)).
- **Fame**: national (Saikaku, Chikamatsu).
- **Play**: walled one-gate quarter = defensible PvP box; rich interiors (lacquer, silk). Set size: district (walled, ~ 250 × 200 m).
- **Src**: Sumiya Motenashi Museum; BUILDING_LIST "Pleasure quarters (yūkaku)".

### Sanjō Ōhashi (and Gojō Ōhashi): across the Kamo river
- **1730**: The Sanjō bridge was the **western terminus of the Tōkaidō and the Nakasendō** (the roads join at Kusatsu). It had stone piers (first 1590) under a wooden deck ~ 100 m long, with bronze giboshi finials on the rails.
  - Travellers left and arrived here.
  - The Gojō bridge to the south was the legendary Benkei–Yoshitsune meeting place.
- **Era**: IN (repeatedly rebuilt on the same pattern; 1590 giboshi survive).
- **Fame**: national (the end of every Tōkaidō guide and print).
- **Play**: the map's west road terminus; a long bridge chokepoint over the river. Set size: single structure + bridge-head plaza.
- **Src**: en.wiki Sanjō Ōhashi; U_URBAN bridges.

### Awataguchi execution ground, Keage and the Hinooka pass: east entrance of Kyoto
- **1730**:
  - **Awataguchi**: Kyoto's main execution ground (keijō) sat beside the Tōkaidō at Awataguchi/Keage, deliberately on the busiest approach.
  - **Keage**: the road climbed east over the Hinooka pass to Yamashina, lined with the blue-flag Awata tea stalls.
  - **Keage spring**: the Keage "kick-up" spring and the Yoshitsune legend.
  - **Cart stones**: grooved cart stones (kurumaishi) eased ox-carts over the pass between Ōtsu and Kyoto. When they were laid is uncertain (a large campaign dates to 1805; earlier ones may exist, verify).
- **Era**: IN (execution ground); the Keage incline and canal are `[not yet in 1730: 1890]`.
- **Fame**: regional (every traveller passed it).
- **Play**: grim fenced ground on the city approach; a pass with a chokepoint. Set size: open site + road.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 粟田口刑場, 車石; BUILDING_LIST "Execution grounds (keijō)".

### Fushimi Inari Taisha: south-east of Kyoto on the Fushimi road
- **1730**:
  - The main sanctuary (1494) and tower gate (1589) stood at the foot of Mt Inari.
  - Merchants were already donating vermilion torii.
  - The mountain was ringed with a pilgrim circuit of stone mounds (otsuka) and tea stalls.
  - The torii tunnels existed but were **far sparser** than today; the "thousand torii" density is mostly a Meiji–modern growth (verify 1730 density).
- **Era**: IN (buildings); REBUILT in character (torii are replaced constantly).
- **Fame**: national (head shrine of all Inari, very popular with merchants).
- **Play**: mountain path through repeated gates = long visible corridor, great for ambush; mountain-top view of the basin. Set size: compound + mountain trail.
- **Src**: Fushimi Inari official; en.wiki.

### Fushimi river port, Teradaya and the sake quarter: south of Kyoto on the Uji river
- **1730**:
  - Fushimi was Kyoto's inland port. The **thirty-koku boats (sanjukkoku-bune)** ran day and night down the Yodo to Osaka from the Kyōbashi landing.
  - The **Takase canal** (Suminokura Ryōi, 1611–14) brought flat boats up into the city.
  - Fushimi was also a stop on the Kyoto–Osaka road and a sake town, with brewery kura along the canals (Gekkeikan's founder from 1637).
  - Boat inns crowded the landing. The Teradaya was one of them; its fame is 1862–66 `[story not yet]`.
  - Hideyoshi's Fushimi castle on Momoyama above had been dismantled in 1623 `[gone by 1730: 1623]`; its hill was being planted with peach trees (hence "Momoyama") (verify timing).
- **Era**: IN (port); the Teradaya building is post-1868.
- **Fame**: national (Kaempfer landed and embarked here).
- **Play**: river hub linking Kyoto to Osaka; boats, warehouses, sake loot. Set size: district.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 伏見港, 高瀬川, 伏見城; Kaempfer *History of Japan*.

### Nanzen-ji: foot of Higashiyama, east Kyoto
- **1730**:
  - A first-rank Zen head temple with its huge Sanmon of 1628.
  - The abbot's quarters had Kobori Enshū's "tiger cub crossing" dry garden.
  - The Konchi-in sub-temple (1630s) held a crane-and-turtle garden.
  - The pine-dark approach had tofu restaurants.
- **Era**: IN. The brick aqueduct is `[not yet in 1730: 1890]`. The "Ishikawa Goemon on the Sanmon" kabuki line is `[not yet: 1778]`.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: gate as a lookout; walled Zen compound with many sub-temples. Set size: compound.
- **Src**: Nanzen-ji official; U_URBAN "Konchi-in".

### Kitano Tenmangū and Kamishichiken: north-west Kyoto
- **1730**: The 1607 honden (Hideyori) was a richly carved hall, the head shrine of Tenjin (the scholar-god). Around it stood:
  - plum groves
  - Hideyoshi's Odoi earthwork behind
  - the **Kamishichiken** tea houses at the gate, among the oldest in the city
  - the market day on the 25th
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national (Kitano great tea gathering, 1587, still remembered).
- **Play**: shrine + tea-house street pair for the north-west; plum grove. Set size: compound + street.
- **Src**: Kitano Tenmangū official.

### Nishijin weaving district: north-west-central Kyoto
- **1730**: This was Japan's luxury silk-brocade district: thousands of tall draw-looms in narrow deep houses (kōshi-front machiya with loom rooms).
  - **In summer 1730 it burned**: the Great Nishijin fire destroyed ~3,790 houses and a reported 30,000+ looms. The bakufu handed out rice.
  - A 1730 map could show Nishijin half-burned and rebuilding.
- **Era**: IN (and literally on fire in the anchor year).
- **Fame**: national (Nishijin-ori).
- **Play**: textile-loot district; "burnt ward" set dressing (charred posts, relief huts) gives a unique, era-true ruin inside a city. Set size: district.
- **Src**: en.wiki List of fires in Kyoto (Kyōhō 15); ja.wiki 西陣焼け.

### Kamigamo and Shimogamo shrines and the Tadasu forest: north Kyoto
- **1730**:
  - These were the two ancient Kamo shrines on the upper Kamo river. Kamigamo has its sand cones (tatesuna); Shimogamo has its primeval **Tadasu-no-mori** forest.
  - The **Aoi festival** had been revived in 1694 after nearly 200 years' lapse and was newly fashionable.
- **Era**: IN. The shrines rebuild by cycle; the present honden are 1863 `[the present buildings are later]`, and the same plan stood in 1730.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: forest inside the city = cover; river water. Set size: compound + forest.
- **Src**: Shimogamo Jinja official; ja.wiki 葵祭 (1694 revival).

### Zen head temples of the north-west: Daitoku-ji, Myōshin-ji, Ryōan-ji
- **1730**: Walled Zen "cities" of 20–40 sub-temples each.
  - Daitoku-ji: its red Sanmon (upper storey 1589, Sen no Rikyū's statue story).
  - Myōshin-ji: a huge enclosed grid.
  - Ryōan-ji: the rock garden (sources through the Edo period describe it; its present form is at least 17th-century).
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national (Daitoku-ji, Myōshin-ji); regional (Ryōan-ji, whose modern fame is 20th-century).
- **Play**: maze-like walled sub-temple grids = close-quarter PvP; gardens. Set size: compound (each 300–600 m).
- **Src**: temples' official histories; U_URBAN gardens.

### Tōfuku-ji and Tsūten bridge: south-east Kyoto
- **1730**: This was a great Zen temple. It had:
  - a Sanmon of 1405, the oldest Zen gate
  - a surviving 14th-century **latrine** and **bathhouse** (1459)
  - the covered Tsūten-kyō bridge over a maple ravine
- **Era**: IN. The Tsūten-kyō is a rebuild of the old form (1961) `[the present bridge is later]`.
- **Fame**: regional-national (autumn leaves).
- **Play**: covered bridge over a gorge = chokepoint; the medieval latrine and bath are fun interiors. Set size: compound.
- **Src**: en.wiki Tōfuku-ji; BUILDING_LIST "Temple bathhouse", "Latrine".

### Arashiyama, Togetsu bridge and Tenryū-ji: west edge of the basin
- **1730**: Here the Ōi/Hozu river left the mountains under Arashiyama's cherries and maples. The long Togetsu-kyō bridge crossed it.
  - Upstream, rafts brought Tanba timber down the Hozu gorge; Suminokura made the gorge navigable in 1606.
  - Tenryū-ji's pond garden survived.
  - The Saga villas and Nonomiya lay nearby.
- **Era**: IN. The present Togetsu-kyō is 1934 `[a wooden bridge stood in 1730]`.
- **Fame**: national (poetry, Kyoto guides).
- **Play**: river crossing + gorge + timber-raft landing; scenic west edge. Set size: terrain + bridge + compound.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 渡月橋, 角倉了以.

### Atago shrine (Atago-san): summit west of Kyoto, ~924 m
- **1730**: This was the fire-protection shrine on the highest peak around the basin, a steep climb of ~4 km. Every Kyoto kitchen had its paper charm. Tea stalls stood on the path, and there was a shugendō presence (Hakuun-ji, syncretic).
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national (fire charm; Akechi Mitsuhide's poem session before Honnō-ji, 1582).
- **Play**: summit shrine with the best view of the basin; a long exposed climb. Set size: small compound on a peak.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 愛宕神社; W_WILDERNESS "Summit or pass shrine".

### Kurama-dera: mountain north of Kyoto
- **1730**: A mountain temple of Bishamon reached by a steep cedar path with exposed root mats (kinone-michi), with the tengu legend and the young Yoshitsune's training story.
- **Era**: IN (buildings later rebuilt; site and path as in 1730; main hall burned 1945 `[the present hall is later]`).
- **Fame**: national (Yoshitsune legend, Noh *Kurama tengu*).
- **Play**: forested mountain path; ninja/tengu flavour. Set size: compound + trail.
- **Src**: Kurama-dera official.

### Daigo-ji and its pagoda: Yamashina, south-east of Kyoto
- **1730**:
  - Daigo-ji's five-storey pagoda of 951 is Kyoto's oldest building.
  - The Sanbō-in garden was laid out for Hideyoshi's great cherry-viewing party of 1598.
  - The Kami-Daigo mountain temples lay above.
- **Era**: IN.

### Imperial villas: Katsura Rikyū and Shugaku-in Rikyū (west and north-east edges)
- **1730**: These were two private imperial villas, both in place:
  - Katsura: stroll garden and shoin buildings, 17th century.

### Uji: Byōdō-in, Uji bridge and the tea fields (Kyoto–Nara road)
- **1730**:
  - The Phoenix Hall (1053) stood on its pond with bronze phoenixes on the roof.
  - The ancient Uji bridge (with its Hashihime shrine and the "Mitsuma" water-drawing platform) crossed the fast river.
  - Tea estates supplied the shogun: the annual tea-jar procession (ochatsubo dōchū) left for Edo from here.

### Yodo castle and the Yodo waterwheels: confluence of the Uji, Kizu and Katsura rivers
- **1730**:
  - A moated water-castle on an island at the three-river confluence, held by the Inaba clan from 1723.

### Iwashimizu Hachimangū and Yamazaki / Tennōzan: south-west gate of the Kyoto basin
- **1730**:
  - **Iwashimizu Hachimangū**: a great Hachiman shrine (honden 1634) on Otokoyama hill, overlooking the gap where three rivers become the Yodo.

- **Fame**: national (Iwashimizu was one of the two imperial "ancestral" shrines; "Tennōzan" meant "the decisive point").
- **Play**: natural chokepoint between the Kyoto basin and the Osaka plain; hills on both sides of the river. Set size: terrain + compound.

### Hirakata and the kurawanka boats: halfway down the Yodo
- **1730**: This was a river post station on the Kyoto–Osaka road (Kyōkaidō). Small food boats (kurawanka-bune) rowed alongside the passenger boats shouting "Kurawanka!" ("won't you eat?"). They sold rice, sake and soup in rough bowls, with a licence to be rude.

- **Fame**: national (a famous river-trip experience).

## == Osaka (Ōsaka / Naniwa) ==

Context:
- Osaka was "the kitchen of the realm" (tenka no daidokoro): about 380,000 people and a web of canals with "808 bridges".
- **The Kyōhō great fire of 1724** (the "Myōchi fire") burned about two-thirds of the city, so in 1730 much of Osaka was **six years rebuilt**.

### Osaka Castle, keepless: north-east Osaka
- **1730**: This was the Tokugawa castle of 1620–29, built over and burying Hideyoshi's.
  - The stone walls are the largest in Japan, with single stones like the **Tako-ishi** (~108 tatami-mats of face, ~130 t) and a wide wet moat.
  - Standing turrets included the Sengan and Inui yagura (1620) and the Ōte-mon (1628).
  - The **Enshō-gura powder magazine (1685)** is solid granite with walls ~2.4 m thick. It replaced a wooden store that exploded in 1660.
  - **The keep base (tenshudai) was bare**: the Tokugawa keep burned by lightning in 1665 and was never rebuilt.
  - The castle was held by a castellan (jōdai) and rotating guard units.
- **Era**:
  - Castle: IN.
  - Keep: `[gone by 1730: 1665]`; the concrete keep is `[not yet: 1931]`.
  - The treasury kura (Kinzō) is `[not yet: 1751]`.
- **Fame**: national (Kaempfer; every Osaka guide).
- **Play**: the west's premier military site. The bare keep platform is a unique high lookout. The powder magazine is the top military-loot room (the explosive story fits DayZ). Gates are chokepoints. Set size: large compound (~1 km across with the outer moat).
- **Src**: en.wiki Osaka Castle; followingtheshogun "Nishinomaru" (Enshōgura 1685); BUILDING_LIST "Keep base without a keep (tenshudai)", "Powder magazine (enshō-gura)".

### Dōjima Rice Market (Dōjima kome-ichiba): north bank of the Dōjima river
- **1730**:
  - Rice brokers moved their floor here in 1697 after the Yodoya confiscation.
  - **In 1730 the shogunate formally licensed it**, the world's first officially sanctioned futures market.
  - Two contract types traded: spot "warehouse rice" (shōmai) and paper "book rice" (chōaimai) for future delivery.
  - The "floor" was largely an open street and riverbank. Brokers shouted and hand-signalled in crowds, with water-sprinklers to break up the scrum at closing (verify detail). Price-signal runners and flag or semaphore relays carried prices outward.
- **Era**: IN (licensed in the anchor year).
- **Fame**: national (Saikaku's *Nippon eitaigura* 1688 describes the earlier Kitahama/Yodoya market).
- **Play**: the economic heart. Could host a server-wide "price board" or trader NPC; riverside crowds. Set size: street district.
- **Src**: en.wiki Dōjima Rice Exchange; ODEX "Birthplace of futures trading"; BUILDING_LIST "Rice broker and rice exchange".

### Nakanoshima and the domain warehouse-residences (kura-yashiki): the island between the Dōjima and Tosabori rivers
- **1730**: The long island and its banks were packed with roughly 90–100 domain rice-warehouse compounds. Each had:
  - a crest-curtained gate
  - a water gate (funa-iri) letting boats straight into the kura yard
  - its own shrine. Hiroshima domain's Itsukushima branch shrine is an example (verify which survive).
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: row of walled rice-and-coin compounds on water = mid-high loot, boat access, many entry points. Set size: district.
- **Src**: BUILDING_LIST "Domain warehouse-residence (kura-yashiki)".

### Zakoba fish market and Tenma vegetable market
- **1730**: With Dōjima rice, these were Osaka's "three great markets":
  - Zakoba: dawn fish wholesale in the west on the Utsubo side of the canal network.
  - Tenma: greengrocers along the Ōkawa near Tenmangū.
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national (as a trio).
- **Play**: food loot, dawn activity, open sheds. Set size: street site each.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 雑喉場, 天満青物市場; BUILDING_LIST "Fish market", "Vegetable market".

### Osaka Tenmangū and the Tenjin boat festival: Tenma, north Osaka
- **1730**:
  - This was Osaka's great Tenjin shrine (founded 949).
  - It was **burned in the 1724 fire** and rebuilt in the following years (verify completion date).
  - The Tenjin matsuri (25th of the 6th month) took the kami downriver in a torchlit fleet of boats. It was one of the three great festivals of Japan.
- **Era**: REBUILT. The present buildings are 1845 `[later]`; in 1730 a post-1724 rebuild stood (verify).
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: shrine + river festival; boat staging. Set size: compound.
- **Src**: en.wiki Osaka Tenmangū; en.wiki "Great Fire of Kyōhō" (Osaka, 1724).

### Tenma, Tenjin and Naniwa bridges (Naniwa sanbashi) and Kōraibashi
- **1730**: These were the three great shogun-maintained bridges over the Ōkawa (Naniwa-bashi about 200 m). They were three of Osaka's dozen public bridges (kōgi-bashi); the other ~800 were built by townsmen. Kōraibashi was the distance origin for western roads and a notice-board site.
- **Era**: IN (wooden; all later rebuilt).
- **Fame**: national (bridge-city image).
- **Play**: river crossings = chokepoints between districts; bridge-keeper huts. Set size: structures.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 浪華三大橋; U_URBAN bridges.

### Dōtonbori theatres and canal: south Osaka
- **1730**: The canal (1615) was lined along its south bank by the licensed theatres: kabuki houses and the two puppet theatres, **Takemoto-za (1684)** and **Toyotake-za (1703)**, with drum towers and tea houses. Chikamatsu, who died in 1724, wrote for the Takemoto-za. This is the birthplace of bunraku.
- **Era**: IN. *Kanadehon Chūshingura* is `[not yet: 1748]`.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: night-life theatre row; large theatre interiors; crowds. Set size: district (a street + canal).
- **Src**: ja.wiki 道頓堀, 竹本座; BUILDING_LIST "Puppet theatre".

### Shinmachi pleasure quarter: west Osaka
- **1730**: Osaka's licensed walled quarter (from the 1620s), with its great gate and the "Shinmachi" courtesans. It was the setting of Chikamatsu's *Meido no hikyaku* (1711).
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national (one of the three great licensed quarters with Yoshiwara and Shimabara).
- **Play**: as Shimabara (one kit, two sites). Set size: walled district.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 新町遊廓; BUILDING_LIST "Pleasure quarters".

### Sonezaki and Ohatsu Tenjin (Tsuyu-no-Ten shrine): north Osaka
- **1730**: A small shrine in the Sonezaki tea-house district. In 1703 a real double suicide of a clerk and a courtesan in its wood became Chikamatsu's *Sonezaki shinjū*, which set off a love-suicide craze. The shogunate banned love-suicide plays in 1723.
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national (the most famous play of the age).
- **Play**: small story site: shrine grove in a pleasure street. Set size: single building + grove.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 露天神社, 曾根崎心中.

### Shitennō-ji: Uemachi plateau, south Osaka
- **1730**:
  - One of Japan's oldest temples (593), laid out on the Shitennō-ji axis (gate, pagoda, main hall and lecture hall in one line inside a cloister).
  - The 1623 rebuild by Hidetada stood.
  - The **stone torii of 1294** at the west gate was the "east gate of the Western Paradise": people watched the sunset over the sea from here.
- **Era**: REBUILT.
  - The 1623 buildings stood in 1730; they burned by lightning in 1801 and again in 1945.
  - The present halls are 1963 concrete `[later]`.
  - The 1294 torii survives.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: classic axial temple with pagoda; sunset viewpoint. Set size: compound.
- **Src**: Shitennō-ji official; ja.wiki.

### Sumiyoshi Taisha: south of Osaka, on the old shore
- **1730**:
  - The head shrine of the sea kami: four honden in a row in the archaic Sumiyoshi style, rebuilt on a cycle.
  - The steep **Sorihashi** drum bridge; tradition credits Yodo-dono.
  - Hundreds of stone lanterns donated by shippers.
  - On the beach, a lantern tower (the Takadōrō) lit for ships.
  - The pine shore was famous in poetry.
- **Era**: REBUILT. The honden date from 1810 `[the present halls are later]`; the same plan stood in 1730. The present Takadōrō is a 1974 reconstruction.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: seafront shrine + lighthouse = coastal landmark; bridge; lantern avenue. Set size: compound + shore.
- **Src**: Sumiyoshi Taisha official; BUILDING_LIST "Lighthouse lantern (tōmyōdō)".

### Sumitomo copper refinery (Izumiya, Nagahori): west-central Osaka
- **1730**: Japan's largest copper refinery (~2,500 m², 100+ workers, ~80 hearths). It refined Besshi copper for export through Nagasaki. It sat on a site almost surrounded by the Nagahori, Dōtonbori and Yokobori canals, and had an underground strongroom for gold and silver.
- **Era**: IN (operated c.1690s–1876).
- **Fame**: national in trade circles; local to the public.
- **Play**: a genuinely industrial landmark: furnaces, ingots, strongroom = unique crafting/loot site. Set size: compound.
- **Src**: Osaka City "Site of Sumitomo Copper Smelting Works"; Sumitomo Group history.

### Kawaguchi and the Aji river mouth: west Osaka, the sea port
- **1730**: Here the canal city met the bay. Kawamura Zuiken dredged the Aji river in 1684 so coastal ships could come in.
  - The sea ship harbour held higaki-kaisen for Edo.
  - **From 1730, sake shippers split off into their own taru-kaisen barrel ships.**
  - There were shipyards, ship inspection, and lighters transferring cargo.
- **Era**: IN. Tenpōzan's artificial hill is `[not yet in 1730: 1831]`.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: the western sea gate of the map (SW bay): ships, docks, cargo. Set size: district.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 安治川 (1684), 樽廻船 (1730); nada-ken glossary.

### Tobita execution ground and Sennichi cemetery: south edge of Osaka
- **1730**:
  - **Tobita** was Osaka's execution ground and a large cemetery beside the road south.
  - **Sennichi**, just south of Dōtonbori, was one of Osaka's great cemeteries and cremation grounds, with Hōzen-ji at its edge (1637). The theatre street ran straight into it.
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: local-regional.
- **Play**: dark edge-of-town site; graves and crematory huts. Set size: open site.
- **Src**: en.wiki Tobita Shinchi (Edo execution ground and cemetery); ja.wiki 千日墓所. (Correction to BUILDING_LIST, which names "Osaka's Sennichi" as the execution ground: Sennichi was the cemetery; Tobita held executions (verify).)

### Kaitokudō merchant academy: Amagasaki-chō, central Osaka
- **1730**: A townsmen's Confucian academy founded 1724 by five merchants and semi-officially recognised in 1726. It held lectures in a large machiya; merchants could leave mid-lecture for business.
- **Era**: IN (new).
- **Fame**: regional.
- **Play**: small "library" site: books, scrolls. Set size: single building.

### Ikutama shrine and the Uemachi temple ridge: central-south Osaka
- **1730**: This was a large shrine on the plateau. Saikaku staged a solo haikai marathon here (1677). The ridge south to Tennōji was solid temple town (tera-machi).
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: regional.
- **Play**: high ground over the canal city; temple-row cover. Set size: compound + temple street.

### Sakai and the great kofun (optional, south of Osaka)
- **1730**:
  - Sakai was a moated merchant city, past its 16th-century peak, known for knives, tobacco blades and (historically) guns.

## == Nara, Kōya and Yoshino (off the main map; side trips) ==

### Tōdai-ji and the Great Buddha Hall: Nara
- **1730**: **A brand-new hall.** The Buddha's head was recast and reconsecrated in 1692 after centuries in the open, and the Daibutsuden was completed in 1709. It was ~30% narrower than the original but still the largest wooden building still standing in Japan after 1798.

  - Nigatsu-dō had been rebuilt in 1669; the "water-drawing" rite was held there.
  - Deer roamed.
- **Era**: REBUILT (the 1709 hall is today's). It stood new in 1730.
- **Fame**: national.

### Kōfuku-ji, the half-burned temple: Nara
- **1730**: **Largely burned in 1717**, including the Central Golden Hall. The 1426 five-storey pagoda and the three-storey pagoda survived. A small temporary hall came only in 1819, so in 1730 the precinct was a partial ruin around a tall pagoda.
- **Era**: IN (pagoda); `[gone by 1730: 1717]` (golden hall; restored 2018).

### Kasuga Taisha and Nara park: Nara
- **1730**: This was a vermilion shrine on Mt Kasuga, with its avenue of stone lanterns and its sacred deer. Lanterns donated through the Edo period numbered in the hundreds (verify count).
- **Era**: IN (honden rebuilt on cycle).

### Hōryū-ji and Yakushi-ji: Nara plain
- **1730**: The world's oldest wooden buildings stood here: Hōryū-ji's Kondō and pagoda (c.700). **Yakushi-ji's east pagoda was about 1,000 years old in 1730** (c.730). Yakushi-ji's golden hall had burned in 1528; a temporary hall stood.

### Kōya-san: Kii mountains, far south

## == Lake Biwa (Ōmi) ==

Context:
- The **Ōmi Hakkei (Eight Views of Ōmi)**, eight poetic scenes round the south lake, were established by c.1500–1600 and are the best period evidence for which lake sites were famous. They are:
  - Autumn moon at Ishiyama
  - Evening snow on Hira
  - Evening glow at Seta
  - Evening bell of Mii-dera
  - Returning sails at Yabase
  - Clearing storm at Awazu
  - Night rain at Karasaki
  - Descending geese at Katata
- Hiroshige's series is `[not yet: c.1834]`, so it is form only.
- The **Chōsenjin kaidō** (Korean embassy road), a lakeside branch via Hachiman, Azuchi and Hikone, rejoined the Nakasendō at Toriimoto. The last embassy before 1730 passed in 1719.

### Ōtsu port (Ōtsu-minato) and post town: south-west lake shore
- **1730**:
  - Ōtsu was the lake's great port. Northern domains' rice (Hokuriku) came down the lake by boat to Ōtsu warehouses, then went by ox-cart over the pass to Kyoto.
  - The "hundred boats of Ōtsu" were a licensed guild.
  - It was also the last Tōkaidō station before Kyoto.
  - **Ōtsu-e** folk paintings (demon-monk, wisteria maiden) were sold as souvenirs at Ōtani/Oiwake on the pass.
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national. **Overlap with LM-E** (Tōkaidō); listed here as the lake port.
- **Play**: lake harbour: boats, rice kura, the lake end of the Kyoto approach. Set size: district.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 大津宿, 大津百艘船, 大津絵.

### Ōsaka pass and the Ōsaka barrier site (Ōsaka-no-seki / Ausaka): between Ōtsu and Yamashina
- **1730**: A short steep pass. It held the ancient barrier of poetry and Semimaru's shrine (the blind lute-player of the Hyakunin isshu), plus Ōtsu-e stalls and the Hashirii mochi shop.
- **Era**: `[gone by 1730]` (the barrier itself is Heian); the shrine and road are IN.
- **Fame**: national (poetic).
- **Play**: the Kyoto–lake pass: a story marker + tea-stall hamlet. Set size: small cluster.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 逢坂関, 蝉丸神社.

### Seta no Karahashi (Seta Chinese Bridge): the lake outlet, Seta
- **1730**: This was the only bridge over the Seta river, the lake's single outflow.
  - It was two wooden spans joined by an islet: the large bridge ~170 m and the small bridge ~50 m, ~224 m in total.
  - It carried the combined Tōkaidō and Nakasendō.
  - "Who holds Seta holds the realm" (Seta o seisuru mono wa tenka o seisu): the bridge was fought over in 672, 1221 and 1582.
  - It carried the Tawara Tōda centipede legend and the "evening glow at Seta" view.
- **Era**: IN (wooden; rebuilt periodically; the present bridge is 1979 concrete).
- **Fame**: national (one of Japan's three famous old bridges; Kaempfer crossed it).
- **Play**: **the map's best natural bridge chokepoint** between the lake region and Kyoto. Set size: structure + islet + bridge-keeper huts.
- **Src**: MLIT tourism DB "Seta no Karahashi"; ja.wiki 瀬田の唐橋.

### Ishiyama-dera: lake outlet, Seta river west bank
- **1730**: A Shingon temple on huge exposed wollastonite rock outcrops. It had:
  - a main hall (1096, worship hall 1602)
  - Japan's oldest tahōtō (1194)
  - the "Genji room" where Murasaki Shikibu was said to have begun the Tale of Genji
  - the "autumn moon" view
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: temple on rock above the river; a view and a strongpoint by the Seta bridge. Set size: compound.
- **Src**: Ishiyama-dera official.

### Mii-dera (Onjō-ji): Ōtsu, hillside
- **1730**: The great Tendai rival of Enryaku-ji. It had:
  - a Kondō rebuilt in 1599 by Hideyoshi's widow
  - a three-storey pagoda (moved here 1601)
  - the famous "evening bell"
  - **Benkei's dragged bell**, an older bell said to have been dragged up Hiei by Benkei and kicked back down
  - a spring whose water was used for the imperial first-bath rite (the temple's name "Mii": three emperors)
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: large hillside compound over Ōtsu port; bell story. Set size: compound.
- **Src**: Mii-dera official.

### Karasaki pine (Karasaki no ikkyū matsu): lake shore north of Ōtsu
- **1730**: A single enormous spreading pine on a small point, held up by a forest of props. It was a second-generation tree (tradition: planted 1591) and was already huge, with its branches spread over the water. A small shrine sat at its foot.
- **Era**: IN (the 1730 tree died in 1921; today's is a later successor).
- **Fame**: national (Ōmi Hakkei "night rain at Karasaki"; Bashō's 1685 hokku).
- **Play**: a single-tree landmark: navigation beacon on the shore; cheap and memorable. Set size: single tree + props.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 唐崎神社; Bashō *Nozarashi kikō*.

### Katata Ukimidō (floating hall of Mangetsu-ji): west shore, Katata
- **1730**: A small Amida hall on stilts out in the lake, reached by a short bridge, founded by Genshin c.1000. Katata was also a powerful fishing and lake-toll town.
- **Era**: REBUILT. Destroyed by the 1934 typhoon; the present hall is 1937. An older hall stood in 1730.
- **Fame**: national (Ōmi Hakkei "descending geese"; Bashō's 1690s visits).
- **Play**: hall over water = an exposed hideout; fishing-town loot. Set size: single building + bridge.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 浮御堂.

### Mount Hiei and Enryaku-ji: ridge between Kyoto and the lake, ~848 m
- **1730**: Tendai's mountain headquarters, in three precincts along the ridge (Tōdō, Saitō, Yokawa):
  - The **Konpon Chūdō (1642, Iemitsu's rebuild)**: a sunken-floor main hall with the "eternal lamp".
  - The Kaidan-in (1604).
  - The Shakadō, moved from Mii-dera in 1595 (Saitō).
  - The Yokawa Chūdō (Yokawa).
  - Dozens of halls in cedar forest, linked by mountain paths.
  - Nobunaga had burned the whole mountain in 1571, killing thousands. By 1730 it was a 17th-century rebuilt complex.
- **Era**: REBUILT (17th-century halls stood in 1730). The Daikōdō and Yokawa Chūdō burned in the 20th century `[the present buildings are later]`.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: **"temple on a peak"** from the FEASIBILITY layout: a spread-out mountain complex, a view over both the lake and Kyoto, and the 1571 massacre as story. Set size: district (three clusters over ~4 km of ridge).
- **Src**: Enryaku-ji official; en.wiki; BUILDING_LIST sect modifier "Shingon and Tendai".

### Sakamoto and Hiyoshi Taisha: foot of Hiei, lake side
- **1730**:
  - Sakamoto was the monastery's service town of retired-monk residences (satobō), with **anō-zumi** dry-stone walls, the masonry style of the Anō masons who built castle walls.

### Samegai spring (Isame no shimizu): Samegai post town

### Kashiwabara: Kameya moxa shop and Mt Ibuki
- **1730**: This was the moxa town under **Mt Ibuki** (1,377 m, the herb mountain).
  - **Kameya** (Kameya Sakubei) was the most famous shop on the whole Nakasendō: a big shop with a giant Fukusuke doll, and a good-luck character in the doorway (verify date of the doll).

## == The Nakasendō: Kiso valley (Magome to Niekawa) ==

Context:
- The whole valley was **Owari domain forest**, under the rule "a head for a tree, an arm for a branch".
- From 1708 the five trees (hinoki, sawara, asunaro, kōyamaki, nezuko: the "Kiso goboku") were forbidden to cut. Timber was floated down the Kiso river.
- Eleven post towns, "Kiso eleven stations".
- Kaibara Ekiken's *Kisoji no ki* (1709) was the period road guide. *Kisoji meisho zue* (1805) is form only.

### Magome post town and pass: south entrance of the Kiso
- **1730**: A post town on a steep ridge, with a single stone-stepped street, stone retaining walls and a honjin, and a view of Mt Ena. Above it, the Magome pass (~800 m) led into Tsumago.
- **Era**: REBUILT. The town burned in 1895 and 1915; the present houses are later. The street pattern is original. Shimazaki Tōson's fame is `[not yet: 1872 birth]`.
- **Fame**: regional.
- **Play**: steep stepped street = vertical post town; the gateway to the mountain half. Set size: post town (~600 m street).
- **Src**: ja.wiki 馬籠宿.

### Tsumago post town and castle ruin
- **1730**: This was the Kiso's best-preserved post town type: a long, curving street of two-storey inns with lattice fronts and deep eaves, a honjin and waki-honjin, and a masugata (right-angle defensive bend). Above it on a spur was the **Tsumago castle ruin**, which held off Tokugawa forces in 1584.
- **Era**: IN (street); many present houses are 18th–19th century rebuilds (the waki-honjin Okuya is 1877 `[later]`). The castle is `[gone by 1730]`, ruin IN.
- **Fame**: regional.
- **Play**: **model mid-tier post town**; castle ruin above = overwatch. Set size: post town + ruin.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 妻籠宿, 妻籠城.

### Nezame no Toko: Kiso gorge between Suhara and Agematsu
- **1730**: Here the Kiso river cut through white granite blocks and pools. Legend made this where Urashima Tarō woke and settled after returning from the sea palace. Rinsen-ji on the cliff above held his Benten statue.
- **Era**: IN, but **the water was higher**: river levels fell from the Meiji period and especially after the 1968 Kiso dam. In 1730 more of the rock was under rapids and pools.
- **Fame**: national (Ekiken, travel diaries; Hiroshige form only).
- **Play**: river gorge with swimming pools, rock cover, a temple lookout. Set size: terrain (~500 m).
- **Src**: en.wiki Nezame no toko; Kisoji heritage site 24.

### Kiso no kakehashi (the Kiso hanging road): near Agematsu
- **1730**: The famous log-and-plank gallery pinned to the cliff over the Kiso river, which Bashō feared (*Sarashina kikō*, 1688). **After a 1647 fire, Owari domain replaced it in 1648 with a stone-walled embankment carrying a wooden deck.** So the 1730 kakehashi was a narrow timber road on a high dry-stone wall, still dramatic but no longer pure planking (length ~100 m (verify)).
- **Era**: IN (1648 form); the stone wall survives beside the modern road.
- **Fame**: national (Bashō; the "three hard places").
- **Play**: **cliff-edge chokepoint**: single-lane, no cover, river below. Set size: structure (~100 m) + cliff.
- **Src**: onkisokaidoroad (Fukushima station); W_WILDERNESS "Cliff plank road (kakehashi)".

### Kiso-Fukushima sekisho and the Yamamura daikan: Fukushima post town
- **1730**: This was one of the **four great checkpoints** (with Hakone, Arai and Usui), set where the valley narrows. The river was on one side and the cliff on the other, with gates at each end. Checking ran "guns in, women out", and here especially women going out. Above the town was the residence of the Yamamura, hereditary Owari intendants (daikan) of the Kiso. The Kōzen-ji temple was here, with the Kiso family graves.
- **Era**: IN. The present checkpoint building is a reconstruction (1975) `[later]`; the site and form are as in 1730.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: **the Nakasendō's mountain checkpoint** from the FEASIBILITY layout; a gatehouse compound at a valley throat. Set size: compound across the road + intendant residence.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 福島関所, 山村代官屋敷; BUILDING_LIST "Highway checkpoint (sekisho)".

### Kiso-Ontake (Ontake-san, 3,067 m): west of the valley
- **1730**:
  - A huge sacred volcano, dormant in the period: no historical eruptions before 1979, but fumaroles on top.
  - Climbing was reserved to those who had done **75–100 days of purification**. Worship was mostly from below at the Satomiya (Kurosawa) and distant-worship points on passes.
  - The mass pilgrim routes are `[not yet in 1730: Kakumei 1785, Fukan 1792]`, and the Ontake-kō confraternities came later still.
- **Era**: IN (mountain, Satomiya); mass pilgrimage NOT YET.
- **Fame**: regional in 1730 (national after the 1790s).
- **Play**: a giant skyline mountain; its summit is a restricted, high-risk goal; waterfall purification sites. Set size: terrain.
- **Src**: onehundredmountains "Mountain revolutionaries"; ja.wiki 御嶽山.

### Torii pass (Torii-tōge, 1,197 m): between Yabuhara and Narai
- **1730**: This was the highest point of the Kiso road and the **watershed** between the Pacific (Kiso river) and the Sea of Japan (Narai river to the Shinano river). Its torii was set up for distant worship of Ontake (its date is unclear (verify)). It was the site of Kiso Yoshinaka's victory in 1183 and of a 1582 Takeda battle. Its tea houses were famous for tochi-mochi.
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: regional.
- **Play**: high forested pass with a view of Ontake; natural choke. Set size: pass + tea house + torii.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 鳥居峠 (長野県).

### Narai post town ("Narai of a thousand houses", Narai-sensen)
- **1730**: This was the longest and richest Kiso post town, about 1 km of street under the Torii pass. It was a lacquerware and comb town: Kiso lacquer, and the famous **Oroku combs** of Yabuhara just south. It had a honjin, water troughs fed by springs, and deep-eaved two-storey houses.
- **Era**: IN (layout); many present houses are late Edo.
- **Fame**: national ("Narai sengen").
- **Play**: **the big Kiso post town**: long straight street, craft loot, springs. Set size: district (~1 km).
- **Src**: ja.wiki 奈良井宿; BUILDING_LIST "Comb maker", "Lacquerer".

### Kiso forbidden forest (Kiso goboku): the valley slopes
- **1730**: This was dense hinoki-sawara forest under Owari guards, patrolled from 1708 under the five-tree cutting ban. It had log slides, timber-rafting stations on the river, and the Niekawa-area forest checkpoint at the valley's north end that checked timber leaving (verify exact bansho).
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: regional (feared rule).
- **Play**: a **no-cut forest** zone: gathering wood here is punished (a gameplay rule with a real basis); timber-raft landings. Set size: terrain.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 木曽五木 (1708); BUILDING_LIST "Timber rafting station", "Logging camp".

## == The Nakasendō: Shinano uplands (Shiojiri to Karuizawa) ==

### Shiojiri pass (Shiojiri-tōge)
- **1730**: The pass between the Kiso side and Lake Suwa, with a famous view of **Fuji over Lake Suwa**.
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: regional (Eisen/Hiroshige form only).
- **Play**: vista point toward the volcano skyline. Set size: pass.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 塩尻峠.

### Shimosuwa, Suwa Taisha and the Onbashira: Lake Suwa
- **1730**:
  - **Shimosuwa** was the Nakasendō post town where the **Kōshū kaidō ends**, and a hot-spring town (Wata-no-yu) with a honjin.
  - **Suwa Taisha's lower shrine** (Harumiya and Akimiya) is right on the road. Each shrine is marked by four giant fir **onbashira** pillars, renewed every 6 years (7 by Japanese count) in the tiger and monkey years. That renewal is the wild log-riding **Onbashira** festival; 1728 was a monkey year, and the next would be 1734.
  - **Lake Suwa**'s winter ice ridge (omiwatari) was read as an omen.
  - **Takashima castle** was a "floating castle" of the Suwa clan, with its keep standing (demolished 1875).
- **Era**: IN. The present Akimiya and Harumiya halls are 1780s `[not yet]`; earlier halls stood in 1730.
- **Fame**: national (Suwa shrine, Onbashira).
- **Play**: a lake basin town with hot baths, shrine pillars (vertical landmarks) and a lake castle. Set size: district.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 下諏訪宿, 諏訪大社, 高島城.

### Wada pass (Wada-tōge, ~1,531 m) and Wada post town
- **1730**: This was **the highest and hardest pass on the Nakasendō**, and the longest stage (Wada to Shimosuwa is about 5 ri 18 chō, ~22 km). There was deep snow in winter, and the slopes are obsidian-bearing (black glass shards, the prehistoric "star-dung" pass). Wada post town sat at 820 m below it.
- **Era**: IN. The shogunate-backed free rest shelters (Higashi-mochiya, Nishi-mochiya, the Settai shelter) are reported as later additions (the 1850s are often given) (verify). In 1730 expect bare pass tea stalls at most.
- **Fame**: regional-national (the road's worst climb).
- **Play**: **the high pass**: exposure, cold, no village for hours; obsidian as a flavour resource. Set size: terrain + a few huts.
- **Src**: en.wiki Wada Pass (Nagano); en.wiki Wada-shuku.

### Mochizuki pastures and the Chikuma river crossing: Mochizuki, Yawata, Shionada
- **1730**: This was the old imperial horse-pasture country, "Mochizuki no koma" of the classical horse tribute. Rivers crossed here: the Chikuma at Shionada needed ferries or fords, depending on season.
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: regional (poetic).
- **Play**: open grassland with horses; river crossing. Set size: terrain.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 望月宿, 塩名田宿.

### Oiwake: the Hokkoku road junction
- **1730**: Here the **Hokkoku kaidō** (to Zenkō-ji and Niigata, with the Sado gold road) split from the Nakasendō at the "wakare" fork, marked by a stone signpost. The present Oiwake-no-wakare stone is a later replacement (verify date). Oiwake was a lively meshimori town, with its packhorse drivers' song (the root of *Oiwake-bushi*) and a cargo weight-check station.
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national (the song spread across Japan in the 18th–19th century; verify when).
- **Play**: **the second big junction**: inns, horses, a fork to the Zenkō-ji side map. Set size: post town.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 追分宿; BUILDING_LIST "Signpost stone (oiwake-ishi)", "Cargo weight-check station".

### Mount Asama (2,568 m), before the great eruption
- **1730**:
  - An **active, smoking cone** towering over Oiwake, Kutsukake and Karuizawa. Ash eruptions are recorded in 1729, 1731, 1732 and 1733, so the smoke plume and occasional ash fall were normal in 1730.
  - The lower slopes were forest and pasture, the upper cone bare.
  - **There was no Onioshidashi lava field yet**: it comes from 1783.
  - On the north side, the village of **Kanbara** was alive. It was buried in 1783; only those who ran up the Kannon-dō steps survived.
- **Era**: IN (active). The Tenmei eruption landscape is `[not yet in 1730: 1783]`.
- **Fame**: national (smoke of Asama in poetry since the Heian period).
- **Play**: the inland volcano skyline. It could double as, or be distinct from, the Tōkaidō's Hakone-style volcano in FEASIBILITY §3 (Stephen's call). Ash fall is a weather event. Set size: terrain.
- **Src**: volcanolive Asama; en.wiki Tenmei eruption; nippon.com "Buried by a volcano".

### Karuizawa and Kutsukake post towns: Asama's foot
- **1730**: These were high, cold, poor post towns on volcanic soil. They lived on travellers and meshimori inns before the climb to Usui, and on packhorse transport.
- **Era**: IN. The resort is `[not yet: 1886]`.
- **Fame**: regional.
- **Play**: the last Shinano stop before the pass: inn-heavy, low food. Set size: post town.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 軽井沢宿.

### Zenkō-ji (side road via the Hokkoku kaidō): Nagano
- **1730**:
  - **A brand-new main hall (completed 1707)**, one of the largest wooden buildings in eastern Japan. It had a T-shaped roof and the pitch-dark **kaidan-meguri** tunnel under the altar, where pilgrims groped for the "key to paradise".
  - The Sanmon gate is `[not yet: 1750]`.
  - Dozens of pilgrim lodgings (shukubō) surrounded it.
  - It was open to all sects and to women: "led by a cow to Zenkō-ji".
- **Era**: IN (1707 hall, new).
- **Fame**: national (one of the great pilgrim goals).
- **Play**: off the main road but huge. The dark tunnel under the hall is a perfect "vault" set piece. Set size: temple town district.
- **Src**: japan-guide Zenkō-ji; Tokyo Met "Edo's legacy" Zenkō-ji (1707, 1750).

### Komoro, Ueda and Matsumoto castles (optional Shinano castles)
- **1730**:
  - Komoro: a castle below the road, whose keep burned in 1626 (keepless).
  - Ueda: the Sanada castle of the 1585 and 1600 sieges, rebuilt keepless.
  - Matsumoto: black-walled, with its original five-storey keep, on the Zenkō-ji/Hokkoku-nishi road.
- **Era**: IN (Matsumoto keep original).
- **Fame**: regional (Ueda: national for the Sanada).
- **Play**: pick one if the inland road needs a castle between Hikone and Takasaki. Set size: compound.
- **Src**: en.wiki Matsumoto Castle; ja.wiki 上田城, 小諸城.

## == The Nakasendō: Usui and the Kantō plain (Usui to Itabashi) ==

### Usui pass (Usui-tōge, ~1,190 m) and Kumano shrine
- **1730**:
  - The pass between Shinano and Kōzuke, a long steep climb from Sakamoto (one of the "three hard places").
  - At the summit the **Kumano shrine straddles the provincial border**: half the precinct in each province, with two sets of offerings.
  - Yamato Takeru's "*Azuma wa ya*" ("oh, my wife"), said here looking back east, gave the east its name Azuma.
  - There were tea houses (chikara-mochi) and views of Asama and Myōgi.
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: **the eastern mountain chokepoint**: steep switchbacks, border shrine at the top. Set size: pass + compound.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 碓氷峠, 熊野皇大神社.

### Usui sekisho: Yokokawa, at the foot of the pass (Kōzuke side)
- **1730**: One of the four great checkpoints, set in a narrow valley below the pass (established 1623), with an east and a west gate and a guard compound. It was especially strict on women leaving Edo, the daimyo hostages' wives.
- **Era**: IN (the site and one gate survive in reconstructed form `[later]`).
- **Fame**: national.
- **Play**: **second checkpoint**: combine with the pass above as a two-stage choke. Set size: compound across the road.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 碓氷関所; BUILDING_LIST "Highway checkpoint (sekisho)".

### Mount Myōgi (Myōgi-san): south of Matsuida
- **1730**: A jagged volcanic-remnant ridge of rock pinnacles, arches and chains (shugendō climbing), with Myōgi shrine at its foot.
- **Era**: IN. Myōgi shrine's present ornate halls are 1756 `[not yet]`; an older shrine stood in 1730.
- **Fame**: regional (one of the "three strange mountains of Kōzuke").
- **Play**: skyline of stone spires; climbing chains = shugendō set. Set size: terrain.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 妙義山, 妙義神社.

### Takasaki castle town and the Mikuni road junction
- **1730**: A castle town with a keepless castle: a three-storey corner yagura served as its keep (verify dating of the surviving Ushitora yagura). It sat at the junction with the Mikuni kaidō to Echigo. It was a big market town on the Karasu river.
- **Era**: IN. The Daruma temple (Shōrinzan) was founded 1697, but the daruma-doll trade is `[not yet: c.1780s]`.
- **Fame**: regional.
- **Play**: the eastern mid-size castle town mirroring Hikone in the west. Set size: castle compound + town.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 高崎城, 少林山達磨寺.

### Kuragano river port and the Nikkō reiheishi road
- **1730**: A post town and Karasu-river port (Kuragano-gashi). Kōzuke rice and goods were shipped by river to Edo from here. It was also the junction of the **Nikkō reiheishi kaidō**, used yearly by the imperial envoy to Nikkō (from 1647).
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: regional.
- **Play**: river landing + junction. Set size: town + landing.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 倉賀野宿.

### Honjō: the largest post town on the Nakasendō
- **1730**: A big Musashi silk-market post town with two honjin. It had the most inns of any Nakasendō station (verify figures).
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: regional.
- **Play**: large inn-town loot. Set size: district.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 本庄宿.

### Kumagaya levee and Kumagai-dera
- **1730**:
  - The **Kumagaya-zutsumi** was a long, lonely Arakawa flood levee along the road between Kōnosu and Kumagaya, notorious for highwaymen at night (verify).
  - Kumagai-dera recalls Kumagai Naozane, the warrior who became a monk after killing young Atsumori.
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: regional (the Atsumori story is national).
- **Play**: raised levee road = exposed long corridor with ambush risk. Set size: terrain (~3 km).
- **Src**: ja.wiki 熊谷宿, 熊谷寺.

### Ōmiya Hikawa shrine
- **1730**: The first shrine (ichinomiya) of Musashi, with a ~2 km cedar- and zelkova-lined approach avenue. The Nakasendō originally ran down it and was diverted in the 17th century to spare the kami (verify date).
- **Era**: IN (halls rebuilt later).
- **Fame**: regional.
- **Play**: long tree avenue + shrine compound. Set size: compound + avenue.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 氷川神社.

### Toda ferry (Toda no watashi) over the Arakawa
- **1730**: There was no bridge by policy, so the crossing was by ferry to Itabashi. It was the last ferry before Edo, with a control office and landing.
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: regional.
- **Play**: Edo-edge water chokepoint (Nakasendō side). Set size: landings.
- **Src**: ja.wiki 戸田の渡し.

### Itabashi post town and the Enkiri enoki
- **1730**: The first post town out of Edo, named after its small wooden bridge over the Shakujii river. It had:
  - a honjin
  - meshimori inns
  - a cargo weight-check station
  - the vast Kaga-domain secondary estate (shimo-yashiki)
  - an old **hackberry by the road, the "cut-the-ties tree" (enkiri enoki)**, avoided by brides; princesses' wedding processions later detoured round it (1861). How early the belief was held is uncertain (verify: pre-1730 detours are claimed but unconfirmed).
- **Era**: IN.
- **Fame**: regional (the gateway), local (the tree, rising later).
- **Play**: **the Nakasendō's Edo gate** (hand-off to LM-E's Edo). A single-tree story prop. Set size: post town.
- **Src**: en.wiki Itabashi; soranews and Japan Today (Enkiri enoki, 1861).

## Top 15 for a condensed map

Ranked for the 12.8 km layout (Kyoto basin west, lake north-west, Osaka SW bay, Nakasendō across the north):

1. **Osaka castle, keepless, with the Enshō-gura powder magazine**: the west's military hero site. Its bare keep platform is era-true and unique, and the granite powder store is a natural top-loot vault.
2. **Kyoto Great Buddha Hall (Hōkō-ji) + Ear Mound**: the biggest silhouette in Kyoto in 1730, gone in 1798. It shows players an "only in 1730" world and has a vast interior.
3. **Nijō castle with its keep standing**: the Kyoto castle. The keep still stood in 1730 (gone 1750). Walls and moats make PvP gates.
4. **Seta no Karahashi**: "who holds Seta holds the realm". The single bridge between the lake country and Kyoto is the best natural chokepoint on the map.
5. **Kiso-Fukushima sekisho**: the Nakasendō mountain checkpoint the layout already calls for, in a real valley throat.
6. **Usui pass + Usui sekisho**: the eastern two-stage choke, with a border shrine on the summit and Asama in view.
7. **Mt Hiei / Enryaku-ji**: the layout's "temple on a peak". It sits between Kyoto and the lake, with ridge-spread clusters and the 1571 massacre story.
8. **Kiyomizu-dera stage**: the most recognisable Kyoto view and a sniper perch over the basin.
9. **Sekigahara battlefield and head mounds**: an open-field PvP arena with hill camps and a strong story, cheap to build (terrain + mounds).
10. **Kiso no kakehashi + Nezame no Toko**: a cliff-road choke and a granite gorge within a short stretch. Both are dramatic and "three hard places" famous.
11. **Dōjima rice market + Nakanoshima kura-yashiki**: the economic engine, licensed in 1730 itself. It suits a trader or price-board system, and the warehouses have canal access.
12. **Hikone castle + lake**: a mid-route original-keep castle town on the inland road, balancing the Tōkaidō's castle towns.
13. **Azuchi castle ruin with Sōken-ji**: the best Sengoku ruin: stone terraces on a hill, with a living temple halfway up.
14. **Tō-ji pagoda**: the tallest wooden structure in Japan and the skyline marker for Kyoto's south entrance. It is cheap (one pagoda) and helps navigation.
15. **Mount Asama, smoking, pre-1783**: the inland volcano skyline. Choose it, or merge it with the Hakone-style volcano (FEASIBILITY §3).

Strong reserves:
- Chikubushima (boat-only island treasure)
- Shimabara or Shinmachi (walled pleasure quarter)
- Narai (the big Kiso post town)
- Zenkō-ji (dark tunnel vault, if the Hokkoku side road is kept)
- Nishijin (a district burning in the anchor year)
- Kōfuku-ji (burned 1717, pagoda standing, if Nara is on the map)
- the Fushimi–Yodo river corridor with Yodo castle's standing keep
- Kusatsu junction honjin
